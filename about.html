A2 · THE COMPANY

An Edinburgh company that got tired of missing the drop.

EdinCard Ltd was founded in 2025 by Fraser Gosling and Teddy Ogosu. The idea was simple and slightly petty: buying Pokémon cards had turned into refresh-and-lose against bots, and it shouldn't have to be.

THE STORY

The queue was the problem, not the cards.

If you've tried to buy sealed Pokémon product in the last few years, you know the routine. Online stock lands at a posted time and is gone in seconds to people running scripts. Shops get allocations they can't announce in advance. What's left sits on a marketplace at double the price.

None of that has anything to do with the actual thing people enjoy, which is standing somewhere with a pack in your hand and finding out what's in it.

A machine fixes the parts that are broken. It's physically in a place, so you can't script it. It's first-come, so being there is the only advantage anyone has. It's priced on a label, so there's no auction. And it's stocked by people who buy through proper trade supply rather than fighting the same queue you were.

So we bought a machine, filled it with sealed product, and started putting them in Edinburgh venues where people already hang around.

HOW WE OPERATE

Four rules we don't bend.

Card vending has a bad name for good reasons. These are the things we decided up front we wouldn't do, and they're the reason the business looks the way it does.

01

Sealed, always

Nothing in a machine has been opened, weighed, mapped or resealed. Pull rates are the manufacturer's. If we can't verify a product's chain of supply, it doesn't go in a slot.

02

Priced on the label

The number on the slot is the number charged. No surge pricing, no hype tax, no "market rate" adjustments between your tap and your pack.

03

It's a purchase, not a punt

You're buying a named item, not a chance at one. No mystery boxes, no random-prize mechanics, nothing that works because you don't know what's in it.

04

Serviced, not abandoned

The failure mode of vending is a dusty half-empty machine nobody owns. Every unit is on a fixed service schedule, and a jammed vend gets a real human reply.

WHAT'S NEXT

More machines, then more cities.

The plan through 2026 is straightforward: get units into the Edinburgh venues that suit them, prove the service model holds up at more than a handful of sites, and expand the stock range as buying power grows.

Trade-ins, singles and submissions come up constantly and they're genuinely on the roadmap — but not before the core thing works properly. A machine that's reliably stocked and reliably serviced is worth more than a machine that does five things badly.

ON THE ROADMAP

  • First wave of Edinburgh installs, listed publicly as they go live
  • A wider Vault-slot range as sealed buying power grows
  • Per-machine stock listings on the site, so you know before you travel
  • Trade-ins and card submissions, once the core service is solid
  • Expansion beyond Edinburgh where we can service properly
